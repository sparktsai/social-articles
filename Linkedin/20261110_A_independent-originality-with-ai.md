# Can an Idea Still Be Independently Original When We Both Use AI?

Imagine two researchers working independently on different systems.

Researcher A treats an enterprise as a human body. Its business systems form a complex nervous system. Each processing point in an enterprise workflow resembles a neuron: information arrives through people and systems, is interpreted or transformed, and then passes to the next part of the organization.

Researcher B studies AI agents. Each node in an agentic workflow resembles a neuron: state, instructions, and tool results move between computational nodes, allowing the workflow to emerge through signal-like transmission—much like bioelectric activity propagating through a nervous system.

The analogy concerns connected processing nodes and the transmission from which larger behavior emerges. It does not claim that an enterprise or an AI workflow is biologically equivalent to a nervous system.

One begins with organizational design. The other begins with AI architecture.

Neither has read the other's work. Both document how the analogy developed and can explain every conceptual choice. Yet they arrive at strikingly similar models: complex behavior emerging from signals transmitted across connected processing nodes.

Did one researcher copy the other?

Probably not.

Did each make a meaningful original contribution?

Possibly—because they applied the shared analogy to different domains and may have derived different implications from it.

But if both used the same generative AI model while developing their ideas, can either prove that the model did not help make the nervous-system analogy available to both?

That is a much harder question.

Generative AI introduces this problem into research, engineering, writing, and design. Two people can work independently, exercise genuine judgment, and still arrive at similar language or concepts partly because they consulted models shaped by overlapping data and semantic patterns.

The difficult question is no longer simply, “Who thought of it first?”

It is also: **What kind of originality can our evidence actually establish?**

## The moment that made me question independent originality

Several months ago, I was developing terminology for concepts in my AI engineering work. I considered multiple candidate words, investigated how they were already being used, rejected terms that carried misleading or overloaded meanings, and made the final selections myself.

AI participated in the discussion. It helped compare terms and explore distinctions, but I did not simply accept its first suggestion. The decision process was human-led: I defined the problem, evaluated alternatives, rejected unsuitable choices, and adopted the terminology that best represented the concepts I wanted to express.

Later, I began seeing some of the same terms appear more often in related AI discussions.

There are many possible explanations. Other researchers may have encountered my public work. The field may have independently moved toward the same language. Recommendation systems may simply have learned what I notice. Or similar models may have made similar terminology more available to many people at approximately the same time.

I cannot determine the cause from similarity alone.

But the observation exposed a deeper problem. Even if I can document that I made the final decision, what exactly does that prove about the origin of the idea?

## Human-led does not mean AI-free

We often describe work as either “human-created” or “AI-generated.” That distinction is useful in obvious cases, but it becomes unstable when AI participates in exploration rather than producing the final artifact.

Suppose a researcher uses AI to:

- generate candidate terms;
- compare definitions;
- surface counterarguments;
- reorganize a conceptual model;
- identify possible prior uses;
- challenge ambiguous boundaries.

The researcher may still make every consequential decision. They may reject most of the model's suggestions, rewrite its language, and produce a concept the model never expressed in the final form.

That is meaningfully different from asking a model to produce an article and publishing the output unchanged.

Yet it does not follow that AI had no influence.

Influence can occur before the final decision. The model can change which alternatives become visible, which comparisons feel relevant, or which framing becomes the starting point. A person can exercise authentic judgment inside a choice environment partly shaped by a machine.

This is not unique to AI. Teachers, books, professional communities, tools, notation systems, and cultural conventions have always shaped what creators can see and express. A camera influences a photographer without taking the photograph. A programming language shapes a software design without determining the application. A familiar biological analogy can also guide two researchers toward similar representations of otherwise different systems.

AI is different in one important respect: it does not merely constrain expression. It actively proposes language, associations, structures, and alternatives that can enter the reasoning process.

That makes “human-led” an important claim—but not a claim of isolation.

## Three claims that should not be collapsed

When discussing originality in AI-assisted work, I find it useful to separate three questions.

### 1. Was the development human-led?

This asks who directed the work and exercised judgment.

Did the person define the problem? Did they evaluate and reject alternatives? Did they decide what the concept meant? Could they explain why the final form was selected?

Prompts, notes, revision histories, rejected candidates, and decision explanations can provide meaningful evidence here. They can show that the human was not merely a passive recipient of generated output.

### 2. Was the idea developed independently?

This asks whether the person copied, relied on, or was exposed to another specific creator's work.

Chronology and search records may help. So can publication dates, citations, browsing histories, and documented transmission paths. But the absence of a known connection is not proof that no indirect influence occurred.

Two people can independently solve the same problem in similar ways. They can also absorb the same public ideas through different routes without being aware of the common source.

### 3. Was the idea free from model influence?

This asks whether AI materially shaped the available terminology, framing, or conceptual associations.

It is the hardest of the three to establish. A conversation transcript can show what a model said during one recorded session, but it cannot reveal every learned association that made one suggestion more likely than another. Nor can it tell us how earlier AI interactions changed the researcher's thinking.

These claims are related, but none automatically proves the others.

**Human-led development is not proof of independent origin.**

**Independent development is not proof of no AI influence.**

**Model influence does not erase meaningful human contribution.**

Without these distinctions, discussions about originality quickly become moral judgments based on evidence that cannot support them.

## Two people may both be original

Now return to the two researchers.

Suppose both can show complete working histories. Every rejected analogy and discarded model is preserved. Every revision is timestamped. Neither has accessed the other's work. Both can articulate why the nervous-system analogy was selected and what it explains in their respective domains.

Their similarity does not make one history false.

The same possibility exists in AI-assisted knowledge work. Two researchers can each make genuine intellectual contributions while converging on similar terminology or conceptual structures. Similar engineering constraints may favor similar solutions. Shared literature may provide a common foundation. The same model family may repeatedly surface the same associations. Public discussion may move an entire field toward a more useful vocabulary.

Originality, in this setting, may not be exclusive.

This does not mean that every claim of independent creation should be accepted, or that provenance no longer matters. It means that similarity alone cannot tell us which causal story occurred.

The stronger the similarity, the more reasonable it is to investigate. But investigation should distinguish at least four possibilities:

- direct or indirect transmission between people;
- independent convergence under similar constraints;
- shared dependence on public knowledge;
- shared influence from similar AI systems.

More than one can be true at the same time.

## A complete record proves less—and more—than we think

AI-assisted work encourages us to preserve prompts, outputs, timestamps, versions, and hashes. These records matter. They can establish chronology, document decisions, and show how an artifact changed.

But provenance evidence has boundaries.

A timestamp can show that a document existed at a particular time. It cannot show that no one else had the idea earlier.

A cryptographic hash can help show that an artifact has not changed. It cannot establish where the concept came from.

A conversation history can demonstrate that a person rejected suggestions and made substantive decisions. It cannot expose every influence embedded in the model or in the person's prior knowledge.

Search records can show that someone looked for prior use. They cannot prove that the search was complete or that an unobserved source did not exist.

None of this makes documentation pointless. It changes the claim documentation can responsibly support.

The record may provide strong evidence of **contribution** without providing proof of complete **origin**.

That distinction is easy to overlook because we often ask provenance systems to answer a philosophical question with technical artifacts. Logs are excellent at recording events. They are less capable of reconstructing the full ancestry of an idea.

## Originality may become a claim with dimensions

In professional practice, it may be more useful to describe originality across several dimensions than to treat it as a single yes-or-no property.

We can ask:

**Direction:** Who defined the problem and determined the goal?

**Judgment:** Who compared alternatives and made consequential choices?

**Transformation:** What did the person add, reject, reorganize, or reinterpret?

**Independence:** What evidence exists regarding exposure to similar work?

**Model influence:** Where did AI introduce candidates, frames, or associations?

**Provenance:** Which stages of development can be reconstructed, and which cannot?

This does not produce a mathematical originality score. It produces a more precise account of the work.

For researchers, it prevents a documented decision history from being overstated as proof of independent origin. For organizations, it supports better disclosure and review without assuming that any AI assistance invalidates human contribution. For creators, it provides language for explaining what they actually did instead of choosing between the inadequate labels “entirely mine” and “made by AI.”

The purpose is not to lower the standard for originality. It is to make the standard correspond to what can be known.

## The question is changing

Before generative AI, creators were already influenced by shared culture, education, tools, and professional conventions. Independent invention was never the same as invention without influence.

AI makes that old truth more visible—and more difficult to trace.

A model can act like an instrument, a reference library, a conversational partner, and a generator of alternatives at the same time. Its contribution may range from irrelevant noise to the decisive suggestion that reorganizes an entire project. The final artifact alone rarely reveals where on that spectrum the process belonged.

So perhaps the most useful question is no longer:

**“Did a human or AI create this?”**

It may be:

**“What did the human contribute, what may have influenced the process, and what can the available evidence legitimately establish?”**

Two people using the same model may both develop an idea through meaningful independent judgment. They may also converge because the model, the literature, and the problem itself made some paths more likely than others.

Both statements can be true.

The presence of influence does not eliminate creativity. The presence of human choice does not prove isolation. And a complete history of decisions does not necessarily reveal the complete history of an idea.

In the age of generative AI, independent originality may remain possible.

What changes is our ability to describe—and prove—what “independent” means.
