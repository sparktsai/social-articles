**What AI product are you actually trying to build, what market will it serve, and what relationship should it have with the product you already own?**

**Is this a chance to reshuffle an existing market, create an entirely new blue ocean, or watch someone else redefine the market around you?**

**This may be one of those moments when every company has to place a major bet on a future nobody can see clearly yet. So how should we bet?**

Most AI conversations begin with capability.

Which model, agent, or company is ahead?

But we can predict that AI will be important and still choose the wrong product, market, or layer of value.

History offers several useful warnings.

Amazon could have kept infrastructure as an internal advantage. Instead, it turned the capability itself into AWS.

Google helped define a new mobile platform with Android, while ChromeOS entered an already mature PC market.

Netscape helped define the browser as an application platform. Microsoft entered later but recovered through Windows distribution.

These cases did not have the same outcome because seeing a technology transition is not enough.

A company still has to estimate:

**Impact** — Feature, product, platform, or industry restructuring?

**Diffusion Speed** — Fifteen years or three?

**Scale of Value** — Existing market or a much larger one?

**Value Location** — Models, agents, workflows, devices, compute, identity, or another layer?

**Architecture** — Cloud, personal device, or hybrid system?

AI companies are already placing different bets across these dimensions.

Some are integrating AI into products they own. Others are building independent agents or becoming the intelligence inside someone else's product.

The remaining bets span APIs, infrastructure, open models, devices, chips, and distribution.

And if AI agents continue to develop, an AI OS could emerge in two almost opposite forms:

**A cloud-centered shell where the device becomes a window into remote intelligence.**

Or:

**A local AI layer where the phone understands identity, context, files, sensors, and permissions, calling the cloud only when necessary.**

Both are plausible.

They create different products, markets, winners, and business models.

My latest article uses historical cases, five evaluation dimensions, and several product and architecture hypotheses to examine one question:

**When a new technology arrives, what exactly should become the product?**
