# When a New Technology Arrives, What Exactly Should Become the Product?

Every major technology transition creates a difficult product question:

**Should we use the new technology to strengthen the market we already own, or treat it as the foundation of a new product for a market that may not exist yet?**

Years later, the answers often look obvious.

We know the Internet became much bigger than the browser.

We know smartphones became much more than smaller computers.

We know cloud computing became much more than outsourced servers.

But the companies making decisions at the beginning of those transitions did not know their eventual impact, diffusion speed, application scope, or economic value.

They could see the technology. They could not yet see where the value would land.

AI may now be creating the same problem.

The question is not only whether a company has an AI strategy. It is what the company believes AI should become.

A feature inside an existing product?

An independent product?

A platform for other companies to build on?

Infrastructure sold to an entirely new market?

Or a solution to a constraint that eventually becomes a product of its own?

These are not simply different go-to-market choices. They define different product boundaries, customers, competitors, business models, and locations of value.

The technology may be the same.

The product can be completely different.

## 1. Strengthen an Existing Product

The most natural response to a new technology is to apply it to a market the company already understands.

A company already has customers, distribution, data, workflows, sales channels, and a working revenue model. Adding a new capability to that system can create value immediately.

This is one reason AI is appearing inside search engines, productivity suites, social networks, messaging products, developer tools, customer-service platforms, and enterprise software.

The logic is strong:

**Existing Product + New Capability -> Better Existing Product**

Google and Meta illustrate the advantages of this approach.

Users can encounter Gemini through Search, Gmail, Docs, Workspace, Android, and other Google products. Meta can bring AI into Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, Messenger, advertising, creator tools, and business communication.

Neither company needs to acquire every AI user from zero. Existing products provide context and distribution that a new entrant would have to build over many years.

This may be exactly the right product decision.

But it also contains a hidden assumption: that the most valuable future market will remain close to the market the company already owns.

Consider a simple thought experiment:

**If Google did not already own Search, Workspace, Android, or Chrome, would it design the same AI product?**

Or:

**If Meta did not already own Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, or Messenger, would it define the same personal AI experience?**

Perhaps the answers are yes.

Perhaps not.

Without those existing products, either company might choose a different interface, customer, workflow, pricing model, or product boundary.

There is another version of the thought experiment:

**What if Google or Meta deliberately built an AI product that was independent of the ecosystems they already own?**

Google might build an autonomous office agent whose product is not "AI inside Workspace," but the operation of office work itself.

The agent could receive a goal, plan the work, coordinate people and software, create artifacts, request approvals, and complete tasks across whichever tools the organization already uses. Docs, email, calendars, CRM systems, finance software, and external services would become interchangeable capabilities beneath the agent rather than the product boundary around it.

Meta might build an independent social-operations agent whose product is not "AI inside Facebook or Instagram," but the management of a brand's entire social presence.

Such an agent could understand an audience, plan campaigns, create and adapt content, coordinate publishing, manage communities, and measure outcomes across many networks, including networks Meta does not own.

These products would still benefit from Google's or Meta's existing capabilities. But they would define the customer problem independently of the parent company's current applications.

That distinction creates a sharper question:

**Would the company allow the new product to create maximum value even when that value is delivered through someone else's ecosystem?**

If the answer is no, the technology is not only being shaped by customer needs. It is also being shaped by the boundaries of the existing business.

This is the first force that shapes product decisions during a technology transition:

**Product Gravity.**

A successful product pulls new technology toward the customers, workflows, and markets the company already understands.

That pull is not inherently a mistake. It represents real advantages.

But it can make one product direction feel inevitable when it is only one of several possibilities.

## 2. Create an Independent Product

The second choice is to let the new technology become a product with its own identity.

Instead of beginning with an existing application and asking where AI should be inserted, the company begins with a different question:

**What product would this technology create if there were no existing product to protect?**

Conceptually:

**New Capability -> New Experience -> New Customers -> New Market**

ChatGPT and Claude are recognizable examples of AI becoming a distinct product category rather than remaining an invisible capability inside another application.

An independent product has room to establish its own interaction model, user expectations, ecosystem, and business model.

It may also reveal demand that would have remained hidden if the technology had only appeared as a feature.

But independence does not remove product gravity forever.

Every successful product eventually creates its own.

ChatGPT is now a familiar product, interface, and distribution surface. Claude is too. As new capabilities emerge, OpenAI and Anthropic face questions structurally similar to those faced by older incumbents:

Should this become another capability inside the existing product?

Should it remain part of chat?

Should it become a separate workspace, agent environment, developer platform, or operating layer?

Suppose autonomous work ultimately does not fit naturally into a conversation interface.

The future AI work environment might be organized around:

**Goals -> Tasks -> Workflows -> Artifacts -> Approvals -> Execution -> State -> Evidence**

Chat could remain one interface inside that environment without being the product itself.

That creates an uncomfortable but useful question:

**Should the next major OpenAI product necessarily be ChatGPT?**

Or the next major Anthropic product necessarily be Claude?

AI-native companies are not exempt from incumbent dynamics. Success gives them users and distribution, but it also gives them expectations, roadmaps, and product boundaries.

The product that opened a market can eventually limit how the company imagines the next one.

## 3. Create a Platform and Ecosystem

A company can go further than building an independent product.

It can create a platform on which other companies, developers, and products operate.

The distinction matters.

A product solves a problem for a customer. A platform changes what other participants are able to build.

Conceptually:

**Technology Transition -> New Platform -> New Ecosystem**

Android is a useful historical example.

Google could have treated mobile primarily as another distribution channel for its existing services:

Search on mobile.

Maps on mobile.

Email on mobile.

Advertising on mobile.

Instead, Google also entered the operating-system layer. It participated in defining the environment in which a much larger mobile ecosystem would operate.

That was a broader product boundary than simply adapting existing Google services to smaller screens.

ChromeOS and Chromebook provide a useful comparison.

Android responded to a new computing form and helped define a mobile ecosystem around applications, devices, developers, and services.

ChromeOS also moved Google into the operating-system and hardware ecosystem, but it approached a mature product category: the personal computer. Its product idea was to reorganize the laptop around the browser, cloud services, simplicity, and managed computing.

Conceptually, the two bets looked similar:

**Android -> New Mobile Platform -> New Device and App Ecosystem**

**ChromeOS -> Web-Centered PC Platform -> New Laptop Ecosystem**

Yet creating a platform does not guarantee that the surrounding market will reorganize around it.

Android arrived while the smartphone category itself was being redefined. ChromeOS entered a market in which user expectations, applications, hardware relationships, and incumbent operating systems were already deeply established.

Chromebook still created a durable product and partner ecosystem. But the contrast shows that a platform strategy depends on more than the breadth of the product boundary.

It also depends on whether the underlying market is still open to being redefined.

Microsoft's experience across mobile and cloud shows how differently the same company can respond to technology transitions.

In mobile, a natural question was:

**How should Windows work on a smaller device?**

But a different starting question might have been:

**If Windows did not exist, what should a permanently connected, location-aware, touch-based personal computer look like?**

Those questions begin from different product boundaries and can lead to very different outcomes.

Microsoft later made a broader move with Azure. Azure did not remain merely an extension of Windows Server. It evolved into a cloud platform capable of supporting many workloads, developers, applications, and technology stacks.

The lesson is not that incumbents always protect old products.

It is that the same company can extend an existing product in one transition and establish a new platform in another.

### Reading Today's AI Bets Before Imagining an AI OS

Before asking what an AI OS might become, it is useful to look at how today's major participants are positioning themselves.

The important question is not simply which products they have released.

It is what future position each product preserves.

Where is the current center of gravity?

Where does the company draw the product boundary?

Which parts of the stack does it leave for other companies?

And can its intelligence become part of somebody else's product, even when somebody else owns the interface, workflow, and customer relationship?

Seen this way, current AI products look less like finished destinations and more like bets on who may control the next coordination layer.

#### Microsoft Copilot: Own the Workflow and Distribution

Microsoft's strongest starting position is not a standalone AI destination. It is the collection of environments in which a large amount of work already happens.

Copilot appears across Microsoft 365, GitHub, Azure, security, business applications, and Windows. [Copilot Studio](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-copilot-studio/fundamentals-what-is-copilot-studio) extends that position by allowing organizations to build agents and workflows connected to their own knowledge, systems, and channels.

The near-term product boundary is:

**AI inside the workflow customers already use.**

But the extendable boundary is much larger:

**An enterprise agent layer that coordinates work across applications, organizational data, permissions, and business processes.**

Microsoft is therefore betting that distribution, enterprise identity, data context, governance, and existing workflows remain powerful even if the interaction model changes from application to agent.

The opportunity is to move from Copilot inside each product to Copilot across products.

The constraint is Product Gravity: the future agent may be encouraged to preserve Microsoft applications as the center of work, even if a truly independent agent would treat Microsoft and non-Microsoft tools as interchangeable capabilities.

#### xAI and Grok: Expand From Real-Time Context Into a Broader Intelligence Layer

Grok began with a particularly strong relationship to X, giving it distribution and access to continuously changing public information.

That creates an initial product boundary:

**An AI experience differentiated by real-time information and an existing social surface.**

But xAI does not have to stop at an assistant inside X. The [Grok API](https://x.ai/api) allows developers to use its models, search, coding, media, and voice capabilities inside products that xAI does not own.

The larger bet could therefore be:

**Real-Time Context + Models + Tools -> Agents Built Across Many Products**

The strategic question is whether X remains the primary destination, becomes one source of context among many, or serves as the distribution wedge from which Grok expands into a more general agent and developer platform.

In other words, xAI can choose between owning an experience and supplying intelligence to an ecosystem.

It may attempt to do both.

#### Anthropic: Let Claude Be Both the Product and a Component

Anthropic is exploring several product boundaries at once.

Claude Chat is a direct conversational product.

Claude Cowork moves toward multi-step work across files and connected applications.

Claude Code applies the same intelligence to a specialized technical environment.

The [Claude Platform and API](https://www.anthropic.com/claude/api) let other companies build their own applications and agents on top of Claude.

Cursor is an important example precisely because it is not an Anthropic product. Cursor owns the coding environment, user experience, workflow, and customer relationship while offering [Claude alongside models from other providers](https://docs.cursor.com/models).

That reveals a different possible center of gravity:

**Anthropic does not necessarily need to own every successful AI interface if Claude becomes a preferred intelligence layer inside products owned by others.**

The product boundary is therefore intentionally porous.

Anthropic can compete through direct products while also benefiting when an independent product chooses Claude as one of its reasoning or agent engines.

The unresolved question is which side becomes more strategically important: Claude as the destination, or Claude as the intelligence inside somebody else's destination.

#### OpenAI: Combine a General Entry Point, Specialized Agents, and a Platform

OpenAI is placing bets across several layers.

ChatGPT provides a general-purpose user entry point.

Work extends that entry point toward longer, multi-step knowledge work.

Codex creates a specialized agent and work environment for software development.

The [OpenAI API platform](https://developers.openai.com/) allows other companies to build their own experiences, workflows, and agents.

This creates a broad possible product boundary:

**General Interface + Specialized Work Environments + Developer Platform**

If these surfaces converge around shared identity, context, memory, tools, permissions, and execution, OpenAI could move toward an AI OS-like coordination layer.

But the platform strategy creates the same productive tension seen elsewhere.

Should OpenAI own the primary experience?

Or should its models and APIs enable the most important experiences built by other companies?

Codex makes this tension especially visible. It is both a product OpenAI can own directly and evidence that sufficiently capable intelligence can become a specialized work environment rather than another feature inside general chat.

#### DeepSeek: Maximize Diffusion Before Owning the Interface

DeepSeek represents a different strategic emphasis.

Its open-source and open-weight releases allow models to be downloaded, deployed, adapted, and incorporated into systems outside DeepSeek's direct control. Its API provides another path for developers that do not want to operate the models themselves.

The immediate product boundary can stop relatively early in the stack:

**Model + Weights + API**

That leaves substantial room for other companies to own the application, agent, workflow, distribution, and customer relationship.

DeepSeek's [open-source approach](https://cdn.deepseek.com/policies/en-US/model-algorithm-disclosure.html) therefore places a different kind of bet:

**The model can become strategically important through diffusion, even when the model provider does not own the final product.**

This approach may accelerate experimentation, self-hosting, specialization, and integration into regional or industry-specific products.

Its possible future is not necessarily to become the AI OS that users see.

It may become one of the interchangeable or locally controlled intelligence layers underneath many AI operating environments.

#### Five Positions, One Unresolved Layer

These strategies overlap, and none of them is fixed.

But their current centers of gravity reveal different beliefs:

**Microsoft -> Existing Workflows, Enterprise Context, and Distribution**

**xAI -> Real-Time Information, Social Distribution, and a Broader Model Platform**

**Anthropic -> Direct Work Products plus Intelligence Inside Third-Party Products**

**OpenAI -> General Interface, Specialized Agents, and Developer Platform**

**DeepSeek -> Open Model Diffusion and Infrastructure-Level Adoption**

The future AI OS may emerge from one of these positions, or from a company that combines them differently.

A company does not necessarily need to own every layer.

But the company that controls **intent, context, tool selection, permissions, and execution** may control the boundary through which every other layer is consumed.

That is the platform question AI now raises.

People often use the term **AI OS**, but that phrase can describe at least two very different product ideas.

The more conservative version looks like this:

**Operating System -> AI Assistant -> Applications**

AI becomes a powerful interface inside the existing computing architecture. Existing operating-system distribution remains central.

The more disruptive version looks like this:

**Human Intent -> AI OS -> Agents -> Tools / Apps / Services -> Execution**

In this model, the user does not begin by choosing an application.

The user expresses an intent. The AI determines what capabilities are required, selects tools, obtains data, coordinates actions, creates artifacts, requests approval, and executes.

Applications may increasingly look like collections of:

**APIs + Tools + Skills + Data + Actions**

The user may know less and less about which application performed each operation.

If that happens, an AI OS would affect much more than operating systems. It could reshape browsers, search, SaaS, productivity software, developer tools, app stores, advertising, e-commerce, and enterprise procurement.

But even within this platform category, there is another unresolved product choice:

**Where should the intelligence live?**

### One Hypothesis: AI OS Becomes a Cloud Shell

If agents continue to improve, an AI OS could begin to resemble the original logic of ChromeOS taken much further.

The device would not need to contain the entire application environment. It could become a relatively thin shell for identity, input, output, permissions, and connection to cloud intelligence.

Conceptually:

**Device Shell -> Cloud AI -> Agents -> Tools / Services -> Execution**

The user would state an objective. The cloud AI would plan the work, select capabilities, coordinate agents, maintain state, create artifacts, and execute across services.

Instead of opening local applications, the user might interact with a persistent intelligence layer that treats applications as remote capabilities.

The laptop or phone would still matter, but much of the product would live elsewhere.

In this version, the AI cloud handles almost everything.

The device becomes the window into the system, much as a Chromebook made the browser and cloud central to the computing experience. The difference is that the primary interface would no longer be a browser displaying applications. It would be an agent coordinating them.

This architecture favors massive centralized models, cloud execution, persistent server-side memory, and infrastructure capable of running agents continuously.

It also creates obvious dependencies: connectivity, latency, inference cost, data residency, provider trust, and the concentration of identity and context in the cloud.

### The Opposite Hypothesis: AI OS Shrinks Into Mobile

The opposite path is to make the intelligence small enough to live on the device.

Quantization, distillation, specialized models, efficient runtimes, and more capable mobile hardware may allow increasingly useful models to operate continuously on a phone.

That architecture could look like:

**Human Intent -> Local AI Runtime -> Local Agent -> Apps / Files / Sensors / Cloud Services**

In this version, the phone is not merely a device containing applications with AI features.

It becomes the personal AI operating layer.

The local model can understand identity, context, private memory, location, files, sensors, credentials, and permissions. It can perform lightweight planning, route tasks, and decide when outside intelligence is actually required.

Cloud models remain important, but they become an escalation layer rather than the starting point for every interaction.

The result could be a hybrid hierarchy:

**Local Intelligence -> Privacy / Context / Routing -> Cloud Intelligence -> Heavy Reasoning / Execution**

This path has different advantages: lower latency, greater privacy, offline operation, tighter access to device context, and potentially lower marginal inference cost.

But it also imposes different constraints: model size, battery consumption, thermal limits, hardware fragmentation, update cycles, and the security boundary around a deeply personal agent.

Both architectures might eventually be called an AI OS.

Yet one makes the device a terminal for cloud intelligence, while the other makes the device the center of personal intelligence.

They are almost opposite product bets.

### Placing Bets at a Table Nobody Can See

No company knows which architecture will dominate, how quickly agents will become reliable, or where customers will permit execution and memory to live.

But every participant is already forced to place a bet.

#### The Cost of Being One Step Late: Internet Explorer vs. Netscape Navigator

The first browser war shows what can happen when companies place different bets on an emerging computing layer.

Netscape Navigator gained an early lead and helped establish the browser as more than a document viewer. The browser could become an application platform, weakening the importance of the operating system underneath it.

Microsoft recognized that possibility after Netscape had already defined much of the emerging category.

That changed the nature of Microsoft's decision.

It was no longer choosing, from a neutral starting point, what kind of Internet product to build. It was responding to a product boundary and market expectation established by someone else.

Microsoft improved Internet Explorer rapidly, made it available without a separate charge, and used Windows distribution to accelerate adoption. Internet Explorer eventually reversed the browser-share gap, but the distribution tactics used in that effort became central to the [U.S. antitrust case against Microsoft](https://www.justice.gov/atr/us-v-microsoft-courts-findings-fact).

This makes the case more nuanced than "the first mover wins."

Microsoft entered later and still recovered because it controlled the dominant operating-system distribution layer.

Netscape saw the platform shift earlier but did not control the layer through which most PC users received software.

The cost of being late was therefore not necessarily immediate market defeat.

It was the loss of strategic freedom.

Microsoft had to catch up under pressure, respond to another company's definition of the market, and use the strengths of its existing platform to defend its position.

Netscape's difficulty reveals the opposite lesson: seeing a new platform first is not enough if another company controls the distribution layer beneath it.

Timing determines who gets to define the category.

Distribution determines whether that definition can survive.

AI companies now face a similar uncertainty. They are not only betting on whether agents will matter. They are betting on which layer will define the market and which layer will control distribution.

A model company can bet that general cloud intelligence becomes the primary interface and that applications become tools beneath its agent.

A cloud provider can bet that agents will increase demand for compute, storage, orchestration, security, and governance regardless of which end-user product wins.

An operating-system or device company can bet that identity, permissions, private context, sensors, and distribution keep the local device at the center of the experience.

A chip company can bet that inference spreads across both data centers and billions of edge devices.

A SaaS company can bet that customers still want agents inside established workflows rather than a general agent sitting above every application.

An open-ecosystem company can bet that users and enterprises will reject a single AI shell controlling all tools, data, and execution.

These bets are not mutually exclusive. Large companies can place capital on several parts of the board.

But they cannot prioritize every future equally.

Architecture choices, acquisitions, pricing, developer APIs, device investments, and distribution agreements all reveal where a company believes value will accumulate.

The uncertainty is not only whether AI agents will succeed.

It is whether the winning product becomes a cloud shell, a local operating layer, a hybrid system, an embedded capability, or infrastructure underneath all of them.

Choosing to build an AI platform is therefore not enough.

A company must decide which version of computing it believes will exist if the platform succeeds.

## 4. Externalize an Internal Capability

Sometimes the most important product is not the application a company begins by building.

It is the internal capability required to make that application work.

### Amazon: The Decision to Build AWS

Amazon needed substantial infrastructure to operate and scale its e-commerce business. One reasonable interpretation would have been:

**Infrastructure is an internal capability that makes Amazon.com better.**

Under that interpretation, better infrastructure creates a stronger retail company.

But another interpretation changes the product boundary:

**If Amazon needs this capability, perhaps other companies need it too.**

Amazon chose not to keep that capability entirely behind Amazon.com.

With AWS, infrastructure that had been understood as a requirement for operating the retail business became a collection of services that external developers and companies could use directly.

That was more than a decision to sell excess computing capacity.

It meant treating infrastructure as a product with its own customers, interfaces, reliability expectations, roadmap, pricing, and economics.

The customer no longer needed to be a shopper, seller, or retail partner.

A startup, enterprise, developer, or entirely unrelated industry could become an Amazon customer because it needed computing infrastructure.

The decision therefore changed three things at once:

**The capability moved from internal to external.**

**The customer moved beyond Amazon's existing retail market.**

**The product moved from supporting a business to becoming a platform for other businesses.**

That creates a different path:

**Internal Need -> Reusable Capability -> External Service -> New Customers -> New Market**

The crucial step was not simply building better infrastructure.

It was recognizing that the infrastructure could have an independent product boundary.

What would Amazon look like today if it had treated its infrastructure only as an internal competitive advantage?

The answer matters because many companies are now building internal AI capabilities that may occupy a similar position.

The product may not be the application currently using the capability.

The capability itself may eventually be the product.

Google's TPU provides another version of this pattern.

Google developed specialized computing capabilities because machine learning created unusual computational requirements. Those capabilities could have remained entirely internal, making Search, Ads, YouTube, and later Gemini more efficient.

But specialized AI compute can also become infrastructure available to external customers through cloud services.

The same company can therefore make several product choices from the same technological transition:

**Gemini -> Extend an ecosystem while building an independent product**

**Android -> Create a platform**

**TPU -> Externalize an internal capability**

This is why company-by-company comparisons can be misleading.

Companies do not have one permanent response to new technology. They choose different product boundaries for different capabilities, markets, and moments.

AWS is now making another version of this infrastructure choice in AI.

It does not necessarily need to own the dominant consumer AI interface. As AI evolves from chat to workflows and agents, AWS can focus on the layers underneath:

Where will agents run?

How will they scale?

How will they access data?

How will permissions be managed?

How will their actions be secured, observed, and governed?

Someone else may build the end-user application while AWS provides infrastructure on which that application depends.

That is a fundamentally different product choice from integrating AI into a productivity suite, search engine, or social network.

It is also a reminder that the company closest to the user does not automatically capture the most value.

## 5. Turn a Constraint Into a Product

There is an even less obvious product boundary.

A company begins by building a new product. That product creates a difficult constraint. The company develops a capability to solve the constraint. Eventually, the solution may become a platform or market of its own.

Conceptually:

**New Product -> New Constraint -> New Capability -> New Product Category**

AI makes this possibility especially important because increasingly capable systems require substantial physical and operational infrastructure.

Compute capacity, chips, energy, cooling, networking, data centers, model routing, identity, permissions, security, evaluation, and governance can all become constraints.

At first, each may look like an implementation detail.

At sufficient scale, each can become a product category.

xAI's combination of models, real-time information, distribution, and large-scale compute illustrates the direction of this question. The future of any particular infrastructure strategy remains uncertain, but the pattern is familiar.

Amazon needed scalable infrastructure and eventually built AWS.

Google needed specialized machine-learning compute and developed TPU.

AI companies now need unprecedented amounts of computation, energy, and operational coordination.

The largest opportunity may not be the original AI application.

It may be a capability required to make that application possible.

This possibility also changes how we should interpret technical bottlenecks.

A bottleneck is not only a cost to reduce.

It may be evidence of unmet demand shared by an entire future market.

## Product Gravity Is Not the Only Gravity

Choosing a new product boundary is difficult even when a company can see the opportunity.

A company does not enter a technology transition with only products and customers. It also enters with pricing structures, margins, sales incentives, investor expectations, and established ways of capturing value.

Those structures create a second force:

**Business Model Gravity.**

Consider SaaS.

For years, one of its dominant business models has been subscription pricing based on users or seats. More employees using the product can mean more recurring revenue.

Now imagine that AI agents allow one person to perform work that previously required several software users.

The product may become substantially more valuable to the customer while simultaneously weakening the unit on which the company charges.

That creates a difficult question:

**What happens when the technology succeeds by reducing the unit on which the existing business model depends?**

The same tension appears elsewhere.

A search business optimized around queries, traffic, links, and advertising may have different economic incentives from an agent designed to provide a direct answer or complete a task without the traditional interaction flow.

If agents select services on behalf of users, who owns distribution?

If software becomes a capability consumed by another machine rather than a UI operated directly by a person, what becomes the billable unit?

Seat?

Token?

Task?

Transaction?

Compute?

Outcome?

This is why two forces should be separated:

**Existing products create Product Gravity.**

**Existing revenue models create Business Model Gravity.**

Both are powerful because they represent things that already work.

They bring customers, distribution, revenue, and proven economics.

But they also influence what appears to be the natural next product.

Sometimes the difficult decision is not whether a company can build the new technology.

It is whether the company is willing to build a product whose success could weaken the product or business model it already owns.

## Five Things We Are Probably Estimating

There is no universally correct product boundary.

Extending an existing product may be the fastest route to customer value. An independent product may reveal a new market. A platform may create an ecosystem. An internal capability may become infrastructure. A constraint may become a new category.

The decision depends on assumptions about what happens next.

At least five dimensions matter.

### 1. Impact

How much does the technology ultimately change?

Is it a feature, a product, a platform, or something that restructures an industry?

### 2. Diffusion Speed

How quickly does it move from experiment to advantage, from advantage to expectation, and from expectation to necessity?

A transition that takes fifteen years produces different product decisions from one that takes three.

### 3. Scale of Value

Does the technology capture part of an existing market?

Or does it create applications, customers, and business models that barely existed before?

### 4. Value Location

Even if the market becomes enormous, where does the value accumulate?

Models, agents, workflows, operating systems, compute, cloud infrastructure, devices, data, identity, security, governance, or a layer that does not yet have a widely accepted name?

### 5. Architecture

Where does intelligence live?

In centralized cloud models, enterprise infrastructure, personal devices, or a hybrid hierarchy of models and agents?

Architecture can determine who controls distribution, context, data, execution, and economic value.

These five estimates shape the product boundary.

Two companies can observe the same technology and make different decisions without either being irrational. They may simply hold different beliefs about impact, speed, market size, value location, or architecture.

## Seeing the Wave Early Does Not Tell You Where the Value Will Land

Recognizing a technology transition early is valuable.

But it does not solve the product problem.

Suppose persistent autonomous agents become the dominant interaction model. Some of today's investments may look remarkably early.

But suppose enterprises ultimately prefer bounded, human-triggered, auditable workflows rather than broadly autonomous agents. The winning product may look very different.

Or perhaps agents become ubiquitous, but the largest economic value does not remain in the agent itself. It may move into compute, identity, permissions, tool ecosystems, execution environments, security, governance, or another infrastructure layer.

A company can correctly predict the technology and still choose the wrong product form.

It can choose the right product form and misjudge diffusion speed.

It can correctly predict the size of the market and still occupy the wrong value layer.

And it can correctly predict all three while discovering that its existing revenue model makes the transition economically difficult.

**Seeing the wave early does not tell you where the value will land.**

History is useful because we know things earlier decision-makers could not know.

We know the Internet became much more than the browser.

We know smartphones became much more than phones.

We know cloud computing became much more than hosting.

We know internal infrastructure could become AWS.

We know mobile could produce Android.

We know specialized internal compute could become externally available infrastructure.

But people standing at the beginning of those transitions could not see the complete market that would eventually emerge.

Today, we are standing in a similar position with AI.

We can see several possible product boundaries:

**Existing Product + AI Capability**

**Independent AI Product**

**AI Platform and Ecosystem**

**Externalized AI Infrastructure**

**A Constraint That Becomes a New Market**

What we cannot yet see is which boundaries will capture the most enduring value.

That is why the most useful product-strategy question may not be:

**Do we have an AI strategy?**

It may be:

**What assumptions are hidden inside our AI strategy?**

Are we using AI to improve a market we already own?

Are we creating an independent product for a new market?

Are we building a platform on which others will operate?

Are we turning an internal capability into infrastructure?

Are we solving a constraint that could itself become a product?

Does our business model support that choice, or quietly pull us back toward the old one?

And beneath all of those questions:

**What are we assuming about impact, diffusion speed, scale of value, value location, and architecture?**

The largest opportunity may still be invisible.

So perhaps the most revealing thought experiment is also the simplest:

**If your company had no existing product or business model to protect, would you use this new technology to build the same product you are building today?**

If the answer is no, that difference may be worth examining.

Because somewhere inside that difference may be a market that does not exist yet.
