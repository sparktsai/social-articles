# Write Medium Stories

Create a publish-ready Medium story that gives a defined reader an original, defensible, and useful contribution.

## Required reference

Read [medium-guide.md](medium-guide.md) completely before drafting or reviewing a story. Treat it as the authoritative platform, editorial, distribution, and integrity guide.

If current Medium features, distribution rules, Partner Program requirements, Publication behavior, or Boost guidance affect the result, verify them against official Medium sources. Separate official statements from strategic inference. Never promise distribution, Boost, search indexing, readership, or earnings.

## Establish authorship before drafting

Medium may restrict low-value AI-generated content from General Distribution. Use AI to organize, challenge, research, and edit the author's thinking—not to simulate expertise or manufacture an article without a human contribution.

Require at least one substantive contribution from the user or supplied sources:

- first-hand experience, case, result, or observation;
- an original claim or interpretation;
- a new framework or decision model;
- a source-grounded analysis that connects evidence in a new way;
- a meaningful limitation, counterexample, or critique;
- a credible tutorial based on verified practice.

If no such contribution exists, do not disguise a generic synthesis as original work. Offer an outline, research plan, interview questions, or explicit placeholders needed to obtain the missing material.

Never invent personal experience, projects, interviews, customers, data, experiments, quotations, citations, results, or credentials.

Infer missing fields when context supports a safe choice. Ask only when the answer would materially change the story or when evidence of authorship is missing. Default to the requested or source language.

## Apply the medium-short length experiment

Until the user changes this strategy, default new Medium stories to a **medium-short** format intended to test whether lower reading cost improves Reads without sacrificing reader value.

Use these working targets:

- English: **800–1,200 words**.
- Other languages: target an equivalent **4–6 minute reading experience** instead of mechanically converting the English word count.
- One core claim, usually developed across **4–6 substantive sections**.
- A short opening that reaches the tension and reading promise within the first few paragraphs.
- Only the examples, evidence, counterexamples, and context needed to establish the claim.

Treat these ranges as an editorial experiment, not a Medium recommendation or ranking factor. Medium officially states that story length should serve the story; it does not publish an ideal word count for distribution or Boost.

Do not shorten by removing necessary evidence, applicability limits, source context, or verification steps. If the claim cannot be established responsibly within the target:

1. narrow the claim;
2. split independent claims into a short series; or
3. exceed the range and briefly note why a longer treatment is necessary.

Remove background that the target reader already knows, repeated explanations, decorative examples, generic transitions, and conclusions that merely restate entire sections.

When reviewing results across published stories, do not infer success from one article. Compare a cohort of similarly themed stories and track:

```text
Presentations → Views → Reads
Feed clickthrough rate
Read ratio
Reads per 100 presentations
Follows or subscribers generated
```

Use `Reads per 100 presentations = Reads ÷ Presentations × 100` to separate reading outcomes from changes in how often Medium presented the story. A shorter story may improve Read ratio without increasing total Reads if presentations or views fall.

## Choose the story type

Select the smallest structure that can establish the claim.

| Purpose | Story type |
| --- | --- |
| Introduce or defend a model | Viewpoint／framework |
| Explain what happened and what transfers | Implementation／case study |
| Use past changes to interpret the present | Historical evolution |
| Help a reader achieve and verify an outcome | Tutorial／how-to |
| Reconsider a belief through lived experience | Personal or professional essay |
| Evaluate alternatives under explicit criteria | Comparative analysis |

Use the corresponding structure in [medium-guide.md](medium-guide.md). Combine types only when the second type provides a necessary logical function.

## Build the argument

Before drafting, create an internal statement for:

1. **Audience** — the reader with this problem.
2. **Change** — what the reader should understand, decide, or do differently.
3. **Claim** — the one conclusion the story must establish.
4. **Contribution** — what is original or author-specific.
5. **Tension** — why the current framing is incomplete.
6. **Evidence** — what supports the claim.
7. **Boundary** — where the claim does not apply.

Split the work into separate stories when two independent claims require different evidence.

Arrange the reasoning so each section follows:

```text
Claim → Evidence → Meaning → Transition
```

Do not introduce a named framework before the reader understands the problem it resolves. Do not confuse polished terminology with an original contribution.

## Draft the story

### Title, subtitle, and preview

Generate 3–5 title directions internally. Select the option that most accurately communicates the problem, claim, result, or useful tension.

Write a subtitle that adds audience, scope, mechanism, evidence, or outcome. Do not repeat the title in different words.

Avoid:

- clickbait and unsupported numbers;
- sensational, mysterious, or generic framing;
- formulaic titles that could describe hundreds of unrelated stories;
- promises not demonstrated by the body.

If a Publication has explicit title or preview rules, follow those rules.

### Opening

Use the first few short paragraphs to establish:

1. A concrete situation the reader recognizes.
2. The missing layer, contradiction, or consequence.
3. The story's core claim or clear reading promise.

Do not begin with dictionary definitions, a complete history lesson, or generic claims about rapid technological change.

### Body

Give every section a descriptive heading and a single argumentative purpose. Prefer concrete decisions, roles, artifacts, evidence, and failure conditions over abstract claims.

For technical subjects, ask:

- Who defines the boundary?
- Who owns and verifies the result?
- Which rule, context, version, or assumption was used?
- What evidence makes the conclusion reproducible or auditable?
- What did the implementation or case actually demonstrate?
- Under which conditions would this approach fail?

Distinguish observed facts, cited evidence, author interpretation, and recommendations. Include counterexamples or applicability limits when they improve accuracy.

### Evidence and citations

Use direct, authoritative sources for material factual claims. Link to the page that supports the specific statement. Prefer primary research, official documentation, first-hand artifacts, and original datasets.

Do not use citations as a substitute for analysis. Explain why each important source matters to the argument.

### Images and code

Recommend a visual only when it supplies evidence or reduces explanation cost. Provide:

- a useful caption;
- source or rights attribution;
- ALT text describing the information conveyed;
- disclosure when an image is AI-generated.

Explain code samples and provide enough context to understand expected inputs, outputs, and limitations.

### Ending

Close by:

1. Restating the claim as a resolved insight rather than copying the opening.
2. Giving the reader a practical judgment, action, or diagnostic.
3. Naming the most important remaining boundary or question when relevant.

Keep promotional CTAs subordinate to reader value. Do not turn the final paragraph into an unrelated sales pitch.

## Adapt content from another platform

When adapting X, LinkedIn, notes, transcripts, or an existing article:

1. Identify the source's strongest claim and evidence.
2. Determine what Medium readers need that the original format omitted.
3. Add mechanism, examples, counterexamples, sources, and boundaries.
4. Rebuild the narrative for Medium rather than padding the original.
5. Preserve the author's actual position and voice.
6. Check duplicate publication, import, canonical, and Publication rules when republishing matters.

Do not describe a reformatted or expanded version as newly researched unless new research was performed.

## Prepare publishing assets

When the user requests a complete publishing package, include:

1. **Selected title and subtitle**.
2. **Two alternate title directions** with genuinely different framing.
3. **Cover image brief** with subject, composition, information purpose, credit, and ALT text.
4. **Recommended Topics** limited to directly relevant subjects.
5. **Publication fit note** describing the ideal readership and submission considerations.
6. **External share copy** that gives one complete insight before linking to the story.

Do not add publishing assets when the user requests only the story.

## Review gates

Revise until all applicable checks pass.

### Reader value

- The target reader and their problem are explicit.
- The story delivers a useful change in understanding or action.
- The title, subtitle, preview, and body make the same promise.
- The story stays within the medium-short experiment target unless the evidence or subject requires more space.
- Shortening has not removed necessary evidence, source context, limitations, or verification.
- The story contains one claim that can be responsibly established at this length.

### Original contribution

- The author contributes experience, analysis, a model, a boundary, or a verified tutorial.
- The story is not a paraphrase or recombination of commonly available material.
- First-hand claims come from supplied facts and are not fabricated.
- AI assistance has not replaced human authorship and editorial responsibility.

### Argument and evidence

- The story establishes one primary claim.
- Every major section advances the argument.
- Material factual claims have direct support.
- Facts, observations, interpretations, and recommendations are distinguishable.
- Limitations and meaningful counterexamples are addressed.

### Craftsmanship

- The opening reaches the subject quickly.
- Headings reveal the progression of thought.
- Paragraphs and transitions support continuous reading.
- Filler, repetition, generic AI prose, and inflated certainty are removed.
- Technical language improves precision rather than signaling expertise.
- Formatting, images, and code improve comprehension.

### Distribution and integrity

- Topics match the actual subject.
- The story does not use clickbait, unrelated mentions, or keyword stuffing.
- Images have rights, credit, ALT text, and AI disclosure where applicable.
- Publication requirements are satisfied when supplied.
- Current platform claims are verified.
- No wording promises General Distribution, Boost, indexing, readership, or earnings.

## Deliver the result

Unless another format is requested, return:

```markdown
# [Selected title]

*[Subtitle]*

[Publish-ready Medium story]

## Sources

- [Direct source links, only when sources were used]
```

Do not expose internal planning notes unless the user requests an outline or editorial rationale. If evidence or author contribution is missing, return the best useful outline plus clearly labeled questions or placeholders instead of fabricating a publish-ready story.

If writing to this repository, follow its existing Medium platform, series, and filename conventions. Preserve unrelated working-tree changes.
