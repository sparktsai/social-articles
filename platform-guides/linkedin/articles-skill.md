# Write LinkedIn Articles

Create a publish-ready LinkedIn Article or Newsletter edition that gives a defined professional audience an original, defensible, and useful contribution.

## Required reference

Read [articles-guide.md](articles-guide.md) completely before drafting or reviewing. Treat it as the authoritative platform, editorial, distribution, analytics, and integrity guide.

If current Article, Newsletter, Premium, Boost, advertising, or analytics behavior affects the task, verify it against official LinkedIn sources. Separate organic, subscriber, and paid distribution. Never promise reach, recommendations, subscribers, leads, or business outcomes.

## Establish authorship

LinkedIn welcomes AI-assisted content but discourages generic, repetitive, recycled, low-substance AI slop. Use AI to organize, research, challenge, and edit the author's thinking—not to simulate expertise.

Require at least one substantive contribution:

- first-hand professional experience, field case, result, or observation;
- an original claim, interpretation, framework, or boundary;
- a source-grounded analysis with a new professional implication;
- a verified tutorial or decision process;
- a meaningful counterexample or critique.

If the contribution is missing, provide an outline, research plan, interview questions, or explicit placeholders rather than fabricating a publish-ready Article.

Never invent roles, projects, employers, customers, teams, interviews, metrics, experiments, quotations, citations, outcomes, or credentials.

## Apply the medium-to-short length strategy

Until the user changes this strategy, default new LinkedIn Articles and Newsletter editions to a **medium-to-short** format:

- English: **900–1,400 words**.
- Other languages: target an equivalent **5–7 minute reading experience** instead of mechanically converting word count.
- One core claim, usually developed across **5–7 substantive sections**.
- Establish the professional situation, tension, and reading promise within the first few short paragraphs.
- Retain only the evidence, examples, context, counterexamples, and professional implications needed to establish the claim.

Treat this as an editorial strategy, not an official LinkedIn recommendation or organic ranking factor. Do not remove necessary evidence, source context, applicability boundaries, or verification merely to meet the range.

If the claim requires more space, narrow it, split independent claims into a coherent Article or Newsletter series, or exceed the target and briefly note why a longer treatment is necessary.

## Choose the format and structure

Use a standalone Article for a complete topic without a recurring publishing promise. Use a Newsletter edition when the topic belongs to a clear ongoing theme and provides value to subscribers expecting that cadence.

Select the smallest structure that establishes the claim:

| Purpose | Structure |
| --- | --- |
| Introduce or defend a model | Viewpoint／framework |
| Explain field results and transferable lessons | Case study／field observation |
| Interpret the present through past changes | Historical evolution |
| Help a professional achieve and verify an outcome | Tutorial／professional playbook |
| Evaluate choices under explicit criteria | Comparative analysis |

Use the corresponding sequence in [articles-guide.md](articles-guide.md). Combine structures only when the second has a necessary logical role.

## Build the argument

Internally state:

1. **Audience** — the professional role facing the problem.
2. **Decision** — what they need to judge, own, or change.
3. **Claim** — the one conclusion the Article establishes.
4. **Contribution** — what is original or author-specific.
5. **Tension** — why common practice is incomplete.
6. **Evidence** — what supports the claim.
7. **Boundary** — where it does not apply.

Split independent claims into separate Articles or Newsletter editions.

Build sections as:

```text
Claim → Evidence → Professional implication → Transition
```

Do not introduce a named framework before demonstrating the problem it resolves.

## Draft the Article

### Title and opening

Generate 3–5 title directions internally. Select the one that most accurately conveys the professional problem, claim, result, or useful tension.

In the first few short paragraphs:

1. Establish a recognizable work situation.
2. Surface the missing layer, contradiction, risk, or ownership gap.
3. State the core claim or reading promise.

Avoid generic trend openings, dictionary definitions, unsupported numbers, clickbait, and formulaic AI titles.

### Body

Use descriptive headings that reveal the progression of thought. Connect abstract concepts to roles, decisions, artifacts, evidence, ownership, boundaries, and consequences.

Distinguish:

- observed facts;
- cited evidence;
- author interpretation;
- recommendation;
- limitation or counterexample.

Use direct authoritative sources for material claims. Explain why each source matters instead of replacing analysis with links.

### Ending

Resolve the claim, give the target role a concrete judgment or action, and name the most important remaining boundary when relevant. Keep promotion subordinate to professional value.

### Newsletter continuity

For a Newsletter edition:

- make the edition understandable without prior issues;
- connect it to the newsletter's stable theme;
- honor the promised cadence and audience;
- optionally preview the next professional question without withholding the current answer.

## Prepare publishing assets

When the user requests a complete publishing package, provide:

1. **Selected title** and two strategically different alternatives.
2. **SEO title and description**.
3. **Cover brief** for 1920 × 1080 with composition, information purpose, caption, credit, ALT text, and AI disclosure when applicable.
4. **Feed share description** that delivers one complete insight before linking to the Article.
5. **Comment prompt** tied to a real professional decision or experience.
6. **Newsletter continuity note** when applicable.
7. **Paid amplification note** only if requested, with audience, objective, and the requirement to separate paid from organic analytics.

Do not add these assets when the user requests only the Article.

## Adapt from another platform

When adapting Medium, X, notes, transcripts, or an existing draft:

1. Preserve the author's actual claim and evidence.
2. Identify the specific LinkedIn professional role and decision.
3. Add professional implications, ownership, application, and boundaries.
4. Rebuild the narrative rather than padding or shortening mechanically.
5. Avoid describing old material as new research.
6. Check duplication, rights, disclosure, and any source-platform rules.

## Review gates

### Professional value

- The audience is a recognizable professional role or community.
- The Article changes a decision, understanding, risk assessment, or practice.
- It provides depth that a Post cannot supply.
- Title, cover, share description, and body make the same promise.

### Authorship and evidence

- The author contributes genuine experience, analysis, framework, or verified instruction.
- The Article is not generic AI synthesis or recycled professional advice.
- First-hand claims come from supplied facts.
- Material factual claims have direct support.
- Limitations and counterexamples are addressed.
- Heavy AI assistance is disclosed when appropriate.

### Craftsmanship

- The opening reaches the professional tension quickly.
- The Article stays within the 900–1,400-word medium-to-short target unless the subject requires more space.
- Shortening has not removed necessary evidence, context, applicability boundaries, or verification.
- The Article contains one claim that can be responsibly established at this length.
- Every section advances the main claim.
- Headings communicate the argument, not generic labels.
- Filler, repetition, inflated certainty, and polished-but-empty AI prose are removed.
- Visuals and code provide information value and accessibility support.

### Platform and distribution

- Standalone versus Newsletter is intentional.
- Newsletter edition matches its theme and subscriber expectation.
- SEO and cover metadata are accurate.
- Feed share copy provides independent value.
- Organic, subscriber, and paid distribution are not conflated.
- No wording promises reach, Boost, recommendations, leads, or outcomes.
