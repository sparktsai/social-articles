# Can an Idea Still Be Independently Original When We Use Different AI Models?

*Human judgment, independent development, and freedom from AI influence are different claims. Our records need to show which one they can actually support.*

In the GenAI era, even when a human proposes the concept and makes the decisions, how can they substantiate its originality?

Imagine two researchers working independently in different fields.

Researcher A treats an enterprise as a human body. Its business systems form a complex nervous system. Each point in an enterprise process resembles a neuron: information arrives through people and systems, is interpreted or transformed, and then passes to the next part of the organization.

Researcher B studies AI agents. Each node in an agentic workflow also resembles a neuron. State, instructions, and tool results move between computational nodes, allowing the workflow to emerge through signal-like transmission—much like bioelectric activity propagating through a nervous system.

One begins with organizational design. The other begins with AI architecture. They never read each other's work, yet both arrive at the same underlying analogy: larger behavior emerges from information moving across connected processing nodes.

The analogy is structural, not biological. Neither an enterprise nor an AI workflow is literally a nervous system.

But the convergence creates a difficult question. Did one researcher copy the other? Can both still make an original contribution?

## Different models may still have a common upstream influence

Now suppose the researchers used different AI models from different providers.

The interfaces were different. The responses were different. Neither researcher saw the other's conversation.

But imagine that both large models were trained on material drawn from the same data lake—one containing a body of knowledge that was especially popular when their training data was assembled.

The researchers did not share their work, and the models were not the same. A common upstream source could nevertheless make the same analogy, terminology, or conceptual relationship more likely to appear in both conversations.

This is a hypothesis, not something the researchers can infer merely from similar outputs. They may not know the complete composition of either model's training data. “Different models” therefore does not automatically mean independent conceptual environments.

The same problem exists outside AI. Two people may read different books that cite the same original theory. They may learn from different teachers trained in the same intellectual tradition. They may independently apply a familiar metaphor to new domains.

AI makes the influence harder to trace because a model can act as a library, conversational partner, and generator of alternatives at the same time.

## Human-led is not the same as influence-free

A researcher may use AI to generate candidate terms, compare definitions, surface counterarguments, or challenge conceptual boundaries. The researcher can reject most suggestions and make every consequential decision.

That work can be genuinely human-led.

Yet human-led does not mean AI-free. A model can influence which alternatives become visible before the human decides among them. It may introduce an analogy that the researcher substantially transforms, or repeatedly favor language already common in its training material.

This does not erase human contribution. Tools, education, culture, and professional communities have always shaped what creators can imagine. The important difference is that GenAI actively proposes structures and associations that can enter the reasoning process without revealing where they came from.

Calling the final decision “human” tells us who exercised judgment. It does not by itself establish the complete origin of the choice.

## Originality contains at least three claims

Discussions about AI-assisted originality often collapse three questions into one.

- **Human-led development:** Who defined the problem, evaluated alternatives, rejected unsuitable choices, and determined the final meaning? Conversation records, notes, rejected candidates, and revision histories may show that a person was an active decision-maker rather than a passive recipient of generated output.
- **Independent conceptual origin:** Did the researcher copy, rely on, or encounter the other person's work? Publication dates, citations, search records, and identifiable transmission paths may help. But the absence of a known connection does not prove that no indirect influence existed.
- **Absence of AI influence:** Did AI materially shape the terminology, analogy, framing, or available alternatives? A transcript can show what one model said in one recorded conversation. It cannot expose every learned association behind a suggestion or reconstruct the effects of earlier AI interactions.

Evidence for one claim does not automatically establish the others:

**Human-led development is not proof of independent origin.**

**Independent development is not proof of no AI influence.**

**Model influence does not eliminate meaningful human contribution.**

## Two similar concepts can still produce different contributions

Return to the two researchers.

Researcher A may use the nervous-system analogy to identify organizational bottlenecks, coordination failures, or places where information is lost between business units.

Researcher B may use it to reason about agent orchestration, context passing, failure propagation, or emergent behavior across workflow nodes.

They share a concept but build different applications from it.

Originality may therefore exist not only in the first appearance of an analogy, but also in how a person defines its boundaries, transforms it, derives consequences, and applies it to a specific problem.

That does not mean similarity should never be investigated. It means similarity alone cannot identify the causal story. Direct transmission, independent convergence, shared public knowledge, and model-mediated influence are all possible. Several may operate simultaneously.

## Preserve contribution without overstating what it proves

If we want AI-assisted work to remain reviewable, we need better records of the development process.

For a substantial research or design decision, I would preserve at least:

- the complete AI conversation rather than selected screenshots;
- the sequence of drafts and version-by-version modifications;
- rejected alternatives and the reasons for rejection;
- relevant search records and sources consulted;
- the model provider, model name, and exact version when available;
- timestamps linking conversations, decisions, and document changes.

These artifacts can establish useful facts. They can show chronology, demonstrate human judgment, preserve the integrity of a draft, and explain how an idea changed.

They also have limits.

A timestamp does not prove that nobody else had the idea earlier. A model identifier does not disclose every training source behind an output. A complete conversation can demonstrate human contribution without proving that the concept emerged free from prior or model-mediated influence.

The record may be strong evidence of **contribution** without being complete proof of **origin**.

That is not a failure of documentation. It is a boundary on the claim the documentation can responsibly support.

Two people use different models. They work in different fields, reach the same underlying concept, and develop different applications.

How would you preserve each person's contribution? Would you retain the conversation records, the version history, and the exact model version used at every stage?

And when you examine those records, what are you trying to establish: human judgment, chronology, independent development, or originality itself?
