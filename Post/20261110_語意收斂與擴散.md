## 四、真正值得比較的是語意，而不是單字

例如：

| 比較項目   | 一般既有用法             | 你研究中的用法                                               |
| ---------- | ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Intent     | 目標、期望結果或行動意圖 | Goal、Intent、Objective、Task 的明確區分                     |
| Evidence   | 支持某項主張的證據       | AI 決策與執行過程的可追溯紀錄                                |
| Boundary   | 系統或權限的邊界         | Model、Task、Visible、Artifact、Behavior、Outcome 等不同邊界 |

## 1. Initial Observation

Approximately six months ago, I was discussing several engineering and AI governance concepts with GenAI.

During those discussions, I considered multiple candidate terms, including terminology related to Evidence and Intent.

The final terminology was deliberately selected by me.

AI participated in the discussion, comparison, and explanation process, but I made the decisions about which terms to adopt and how they should be defined within my conceptual frameworks.

Over the following months, I incorporated these terms into research papers, technical articles, social media posts, and engineering discussions.

Recently, I noticed that similar terminology appeared increasingly often in discussions about AI agents, AI-assisted software development, governance, and related engineering practices.

This observation raised a question:

**Why do terms that once appeared relatively uncommon in a specific technical context now seem to appear everywhere?**

Several explanations are possible.

Perhaps the terminology was already strongly represented in the model's training data.

Perhaps different researchers independently arrived at similar conceptual choices.

Perhaps public discussions contributed to terminology diffusion.

Or perhaps recommendation algorithms and selective attention simply made the terminology more visible to me.

The observation alone cannot establish which explanation is correct.

---

## 2. Human-Led Terminology Selection

An important detail is that the terminology was not simply accepted from AI suggestions.

The selection process included:

1. Identifying the concept that needed to be expressed.
2. Generating or examining multiple candidate terms.
3. Researching existing terminology on the internet.
4. Investigating established meanings in other domains.
5. Avoiding terms that were already heavily used in potentially conflicting contexts.
6. Comparing semantic precision and ambiguity.
7. Selecting terminology through human judgment.
8. Defining the selected terms within a specific conceptual framework.
9. Using the terminology consistently in subsequent publications.

The intention was not necessarily to invent a new English word.

It was to select terminology that could represent a specific engineering concept without inheriting unnecessary ambiguity from existing technical usage.

This distinction matters.

**Choosing an existing word for a new conceptual role is different from inventing the word itself.**

Likewise, human-led terminology selection does not establish that the selected word or its underlying concept had never appeared elsewhere.

---

## 3. The Evidence Example

Evidence is one representative example.

At the time of the original terminology investigation, I observed that Evidence was strongly associated with legal, evidentiary, and related contexts.

In the AI development tools and agentic systems I examined, terminology such as Log, Trace, Telemetry, History, and Audit Record appeared more commonly in discussions of system behavior and execution records.

I did not observe Evidence being used as broadly in the specific AI engineering context I was investigating.

This was an observation from a particular research period and search scope, not a verified claim about the entire industry.

The semantic distinction was important.

- **Log:** What was recorded?
- **Trace:** What execution path occurred?
- **Telemetry:** What operational signals were observed?
- **Audit Record:** What records are available for inspection?
- **Evidence:** What information supports a particular claim, decision, or conclusion?

Evidence can potentially incorporate information from logs, traces, artifacts, and other records.

However, those records do not automatically become sufficient evidence merely because they exist.

Evidence introduces questions of relevance, provenance, reliability, and the relationship between a claim and the information supporting it.

This distinction made the term useful in discussions of AI-assisted development and engineering governance.

Later, I began seeing Evidence appear more frequently in related AI discussions.

The question is whether this reflects actual terminology migration, independent conceptual convergence, social diffusion, or changes in personal exposure.

Evidence was already used in fields such as law, medicine, software engineering, security, and auditing. The relevant research question concerns its adoption and changing meaning within a narrower AI engineering context.

---

## 4. The Intent Example

Intent presents a related but different case.

The term already existed in multiple technical fields, including intent-based networking, software systems, and natural language understanding.

The relevant question was not whether Intent was a new word.

It was whether Intent could serve a distinct conceptual role in a structured engineering framework.

For example, an engineering model may distinguish:

- Goal
- Intent
- Objective
- Task

These concepts are related but are not necessarily interchangeable.

A Goal may describe a desired end state.

Intent may describe the intended direction, purpose, or meaning behind an action.

An Objective may express a more specific target.

A Task may describe an executable unit of work.

These distinctions are framework-dependent and should not be presented as universal definitions.

The research question is whether different people independently develop similar distinctions, adopt them through communication, or converge because shared AI models and technical discussions encourage similar terminology.

---

## 5. The Original Hypothesis: Model Preference or Semantic Diffusion?

The first explanation considered was model preference.

Could Evidence and Intent have been strongly represented in the model's training data?

If so, the model might repeatedly suggest these terms when users discuss related engineering problems.

However, language models do not simply assign a single fixed importance score to every word.

Their outputs depend on learned relationships, contextual information, model architecture, training data, and generation conditions.

Another possibility was semantic diffusion.

Could repeated discussions with AI, followed by public research papers and articles, contribute to the spread of terminology?

These possibilities must be separated.

### 5.1 Within-Conversation Influence

Repeated terminology within a conversation can influence later model responses through context.

This does not mean the model's underlying parameters have been updated.

### 5.2 Cross-User Influence

One user's private conversation does not ordinarily modify the shared base model in real time for other users.

The possibility that eligible conversations may be used in future model improvement is different from immediate cross-user propagation.

Without evidence about training inclusion and subsequent model behavior, influence cannot be attributed to a particular private conversation.

### 5.3 Public Content Diffusion

Research papers, technical articles, GitHub repositories, LinkedIn posts, and X discussions can be encountered by other people.

Those people may then introduce similar concepts into their own discussions with AI.

This creates a possible human-mediated diffusion pathway.

However, a possible pathway is not proof that diffusion occurred.

---

## 6. Survivorship Bias and Selective Exposure

Another explanation emerged during the discussion.

Perhaps the apparent increase in terminology usage reflects a form of observational bias.

After deliberately selecting Evidence or Intent, I may have become more sensitive to those words.

At the same time, publishing related content and interacting with researchers in the same field may have changed the information shown to me by social platforms.

Several mechanisms are relevant.

### Frequency Illusion

Once a term becomes personally meaningful, occurrences of that term become easier to notice.

### Selective Exposure

Users may encounter increasing amounts of content aligned with their existing interests.

### Algorithmic Recommendation

Platforms may recommend content based on interactions, follows, engagement patterns, and inferred interests.

### Homophily

People with similar professional interests tend to connect with and interact with one another.

### Survivorship Bias

If I examine only the terms I selected, while ignoring rejected alternatives, I may overestimate the significance of their later popularity.

These mechanisms can operate simultaneously.

An increase in observed terminology does not necessarily imply an equivalent increase in global usage.

The distinction is between:

**Actual prevalence and observed exposure.**

## 7. Three Research Directions

The discussion developed into three connected research directions.

### 7.1 Conceptual Convergence

**Research question:** Why do independent researchers arrive at similar concepts or terminology?

Possible mechanisms include:

- Similar engineering problems.
- Shared technical knowledge.
- Common conceptual constraints.
- Existing disciplinary vocabulary.
- Similar AI model behavior.
- Independent reasoning toward comparable solutions.

Conceptual convergence does not require direct communication.

Two researchers may independently recognize the same engineering problem and develop similar abstractions.

A shared term alone is insufficient to demonstrate shared conceptual meaning.

### 7.2 Algorithmic Exposure and Social Network Diffusion

**Research question:** How do concepts become visible and spread through social and technical networks?

Potential mechanisms include:

- Platform recommendation algorithms.
- Social network connections.
- Professional communities.
- Reposts and quotations.
- Academic citations.
- Public repositories.
- AI-assisted content generation.
- Repeated exposure through overlapping audiences.

Exposure and adoption are separate events.

A person may encounter a term without understanding, accepting, or reusing it.

Social network diffusion also does not necessarily imply that the original source can be identified.

### 7.3 Semantic Alignment and Adoption

**Research question:** How does communication cause people to adopt shared terminology and meaning?

A conversation may involve several stages:

1. Encountering a term.
2. Interpreting its meaning.
3. Comparing it with existing terminology.
4. Negotiating or clarifying the definition.
5. Accepting the terminology.
6. Reusing it in another context.
7. Transmitting it to additional participants.

This connects with existing research on lexical entrainment, conceptual pacts, common ground, and linguistic conventions.

However, three outcomes must be distinguished:

**Lexical Adoption:** Two people use the same word.

**Semantic Alignment:** Two people use the word with substantially similar meanings.

**Conceptual Agreement:** Two people accept substantially similar underlying claims or models.

These are not equivalent.

A person may adopt a word while assigning it a different meaning.

Conversely, two people may share the same concept while using different terminology.

## 8. A Combined Diffusion Model

The three research directions can be connected into a provisional process model.

Concept Formation  
→ Terminology Selection  
→ Publication or Communication  
→ Algorithmic and Social Exposure  
→ Semantic Interpretation  
→ Acceptance or Rejection  
→ Terminology Reuse  
→ Further Diffusion

The model includes feedback loops.

Once terminology is reused, it may become more visible through search engines, social networks, public documents, and AI-assisted communication.

However, this sequence should not be interpreted as a universal causal pathway.

Independent convergence may occur without exposure.

Exposure may occur without adoption.

Adoption may occur without semantic agreement.

And increased visibility may occur without an increase in actual adoption.

The combined framework is a research hypothesis, not an established theory.

## 9. The GenAI Originality Question

The discussion then moved beyond terminology diffusion.

A more fundamental question emerged:

**In the GenAI era, how can we determine whether terminology deliberately chosen by a human was genuinely independent of AI influence?**

Consider a researcher who:

- Defines a problem.
- Compares candidate terminology.
- Searches for prior usage.
- Rejects ambiguous terms.
- Makes the final decision.
- Documents the reasoning.
- Publishes the resulting concept.

This is a human-led process.

But the researcher may still have been influenced by AI-generated suggestions, explanations, comparisons, or framing.

Even when the final decision is made by a human, the cognitive environment may include AI-generated information.

This introduces an important distinction:

**Human-Led Decision-Making is not equivalent to Absence of AI Influence.**

Evidence of human contribution can support a claim that the person exercised meaningful judgment.

It cannot automatically establish that the underlying idea developed without external or model-mediated influence.

## 10. Anonymized IP-Related Scenario

A related intellectual property discussion introduced another concern.

Consider a researcher who develops a conceptual framework through a series of human-led AI conversations.

The researcher records decisions, rejects alternatives, revises definitions, and eventually publishes the work.

Later, another AI-assisted publication or technical artifact appears with similar terminology or conceptual structures.

The similarity raises questions:

Did one party encounter the other's work?

Did both independently reach similar conclusions?

Did both rely on similar public sources?

Did the models encourage comparable terminology?

Could one model have been influenced by material from an earlier publication?

The scenario does not establish copying, unauthorized disclosure, infringement, or misconduct.

Similarity alone cannot determine causation.

It also cannot establish whether confidential information was transferred between systems.

The scenario is useful because it reveals a limitation of process evidence.

A detailed human decision record can demonstrate how a person developed and selected an idea.

It does not necessarily establish the complete origin of every concept, association, or suggestion encountered during that process.

---

## 11. Two Human-Led Researchers, One Similar Idea

The next extension is more challenging.

Imagine two researchers, A and B.

Both independently document their work.

Both define an engineering problem.

Both compare candidate terms.

Both examine existing terminology.

Both reject alternatives.

Both select the same word.

Both develop similar conceptual structures.

Both use GenAI during their research.

Both can provide evidence that the final decisions were made by humans.

Can both claim human-led originality?

Potentially, yes.

Human-led development is not inherently exclusive.

Two people can independently contribute original reasoning to similar solutions.

But the situation becomes more complex when both use similar AI models.

The models may share training sources, learned semantic associations, and common terminology preferences.

The researchers may also share public technical knowledge without being aware of the same specific sources.

Their reasoning processes can be genuinely human-led while still being influenced by common external information.

This creates three distinct claims.

### Claim A: Human-Led Development

The human actively directed, evaluated, rejected, and selected conceptual elements.

### Claim B: Independent Development

The concept was developed without copying or relying on the other researcher's work.

### Claim C: Absence of Model Influence

The model did not materially influence the terminology or concept.

Evidence supporting Claim A does not automatically establish Claim B or Claim C.

Two researchers may both satisfy Claim A while the evidence remains insufficient to determine Claims B and C.

The difficulty is not simply deciding which person was first.

It is determining what the available evidence can legitimately establish.

## 12. The Shared Model Influence Problem

Suppose researchers A and B use the same underlying model family.

Both ask the model to analyze similar engineering problems.

Both independently make final terminology decisions.

Both select Evidence.

There are several possible explanations:

**Scenario 1: Independent Human Convergence**

Both researchers independently reason that Evidence best represents the concept.

**Scenario 2: Shared Model Preference**

The model presents or favors Evidence in both discussions, influencing the available choices.

**Scenario 3: Shared External Knowledge**

Both researchers and models draw on overlapping technical literature.

**Scenario 4: Social Diffusion**

One researcher encounters terminology originating from the other, directly or indirectly.

**Scenario 5: Combined Influence**

Human reasoning, model behavior, public information, and social communication jointly contribute to the outcome.

These scenarios are not always mutually exclusive.

A model may influence the framing while a human independently develops the final definition.

A researcher may encounter an existing term but create a distinct engineering application.

Two researchers may independently create similar concepts because the same engineering constraints favor similar solutions.

The presence of model influence does not automatically negate meaningful human originality.

Likewise, human-led decisions do not establish complete conceptual independence.

## 13. Evidence of Contribution Versus Proof of Origin

This discussion creates a distinction between evidence of human contribution and evidence of independent origin.

A research process may preserve:

- Original prompts.
- Candidate terminology.
- Rejected alternatives.
- Search results.
- Decision explanations.
- Model identifiers.
- Conversation timestamps.
- Document revisions.
- Version history.
- Publication records.
- Cryptographic hashes.

These records can support claims about chronology, decision-making, and the integrity of preserved artifacts.

However, they have limitations.

A timestamp does not prove an idea was unknown elsewhere.

A hash can help establish that an artifact has not changed since a particular recorded state, but it does not establish conceptual originality.

A conversation transcript may show human decision-making without revealing all influences from model training or external sources.

A model identifier does not reveal which training examples contributed to a specific output.

Therefore:

**Evidence of Human Contribution ≠ Proof of Independent Origin**

And:

**Human-Led Originality ≠ Proof of No AI Influence**

This is an epistemic distinction, not a conclusion about legal ownership or patentability.

Legal standards for authorship, copyright, patents, and trade secrets vary by jurisdiction and cannot be inferred solely from these research concepts.

## 14. Competing Research Hypotheses

The discussion produced six provisional hypotheses.

### H1 — Independent Conceptual Convergence

Researchers facing similar problems may independently adopt similar terminology and conceptual structures.

### H2 — Social Semantic Diffusion

Public research, articles, repositories, and interpersonal communication may influence terminology adoption.

### H3 — Algorithmic Exposure Amplification

Recommendation systems and network interactions may increase perceived terminology prevalence without equivalent changes in overall usage.

### H4 — Common External Drivers

Shared technological developments, such as the rise of agentic systems and AI governance, may independently encourage similar terminology choices.

### H5 — Human–AI Mediated Semantic Convergence

Human decisions, AI-assisted reasoning, public communication, and social feedback may jointly contribute to the convergence of terminology and meaning.

### H6 — Shared Model Influence Under Human-Led Decisions

Two independently documented human-led processes may converge partly because their AI systems share learned semantic preferences or sources.

None of these hypotheses is established merely by observing similar terminology.

They require different forms of evidence and may operate simultaneously.

## 15. Historical Evidence and Research Design

The original terminology selection process may provide a useful historical baseline.

A possible research design would compare four periods.

### T0 — Before Terminology Selection

Examine the candidate terms, original search results, existing usage, and semantic conflicts.

### T1 — Human Selection and Initial Publication

Examine conversation records, decision rationale, first documented usage, and publication dates.

### T2 — Social Exposure and Interaction

Examine citations, replies, reposts, professional networks, and discussions involving the terminology.

### T3 — Subsequent Adoption

Examine later uses of the terminology, the associated definitions, and any traceable relationship to earlier publications.

Important comparisons include:

- Selected terms versus rejected candidate terms.
- Public usage versus personally recommended content.
- Exact word matches versus semantic similarity.
- Shared terminology versus shared conceptual structure.
- Authors with documented exposure versus authors without known exposure.
- Human-led decisions versus model-generated candidate suggestions.

Historical search results may be incomplete, and the absence of an observed term does not establish its absence from the wider literature.

The investigation must also distinguish prior publication from prior invention and avoid inferring causality from chronology alone.

A useful finding would be that terminology usage changed significantly within a defined domain.

A stronger finding would identify semantic alignment.

An even stronger finding would identify plausible and documented transmission paths.

No single observation would necessarily prove the complete origin of an idea.

## 18. Research Position and Boundaries

This discussion does not claim that Evidence or Intent originated with me.

It does not claim that AI companies or other researchers adopted terminology from my publications.

It does not claim that private AI conversations directly altered shared model parameters.

It does not establish that the terminology has objectively increased in usage across the industry.

It does not claim that similar AI-generated outputs demonstrate intellectual property infringement.

It does not treat human-led decision-making as proof of complete independence from model influence.

The documented personal observation is narrower:

I deliberately selected terminology through human judgment, after considering alternatives and investigating existing usage.

I subsequently published and discussed those terms.

I later observed similar terminology appearing more frequently in related AI engineering discussions.

The mechanisms behind that observation remain open research questions.

The broader research proposition is that GenAI introduces another potential source of semantic convergence into an environment already shaped by human reasoning, professional networks, public knowledge, and platform recommendation systems.

The originality question is not only who selected a term first.

It is what we can legitimately infer from the evidence of how people selected, defined, and communicated their ideas.

## 20. Final Research Proposition

The central issue is not simply whether AI suggested a particular word.

Nor is it whether multiple researchers eventually used the same terminology.

The deeper question concerns how concepts emerge, converge, and spread when human reasoning, AI models, public knowledge, social networks, and recommendation algorithms interact.

In this environment, two researchers may independently exercise meaningful human judgment and still arrive at similar concepts.

Both may have legitimate human contributions.

Both may also be influenced by common model behavior or shared knowledge.

Their documented decision histories can establish aspects of their contributions without necessarily resolving the independent origin of the ideas.

**Human-led development, independent conceptual origin, and absence of AI influence are three different claims.**

Understanding the distinction may become increasingly important as GenAI becomes embedded in research, engineering, publishing, and intellectual work.
